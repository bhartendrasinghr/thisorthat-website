Ask mutual fund investors what they want to know and one question comes up again and again:

**Which is the best mutual fund to invest in?**

I asked Gajendra Kothari something similar during our conversation on the *This or That Podcast*. His answer was refreshingly simple:

> **“Best fund hota hi nahi hai. Best investor hota hai.”**

That line stayed with me.

Because we spend an extraordinary amount of time looking for the right fund, the right category and the right entry point. But much less time thinking about whether our own behaviour allows any investment strategy to actually work.

Here are seven ideas from the conversation that I found particularly useful.

## 1. Returns are only one part of wealth creation

Investors naturally focus on returns.

Can I make 12%?
Can I make 15%?
Which fund will outperform?

But Gajendra's point was that there are two other variables that matter enormously:

**How much money can you invest? And For how long can you stay invested?**

His own journey started with a ₹10,000 monthly SIP. At the time, he says, he wasn't even sure he would have the conviction to continue it for 16 years.

He simply kept going. Over long periods, this starts changing the mathematics dramatically.

We cannot decide what return the market will give us next year. But how much we save, how consistently we invest and how much time we give that money are considerably more within our control.

## 2. Don't come in the way of compounding

One of my favourite lines from the conversation was:

**“Don't come in the way of compounding.”**

It sounds obvious until you look at how most of us actually invest.Markets fall, we stop SIPs. Another fund does better, we switch. Gold starts performing, we move there. Small caps rally, suddenly we want small caps.We constantly feel that we should be doing something. Gajendra says much of his own wealth was created precisely because he did less. He started SIPs, continued them and increased them over time.

He also made an interesting observation: the fewer unnecessary entries there are in an investor's portfolio statement, the better.

Activity and progress are not necessarily the same thing.

## 3. Poor returns may be the worst reason to stop an SIP

This was probably the sharpest part of the conversation.

Suppose you have been investing for three years and returns have been disappointing.

Your instinct may be to stop the SIP. Gajendra's argument is that this can be precisely the wrong moment.

You have already lived through the weak part of the cycle. Then, because another fund or asset class has recently done well, you take your money out and move to the recent winner.

A couple of years later, the cycle changes again. Then you switch again.

This is how investors can spend years chasing performance without ever staying anywhere long enough to benefit from the full cycle.As Gajendra memorably put it:

**“Lakshmi tika lagane aa rahi hai aur aap muh dhone ja rahe ho.”**

That doesn't mean a bad fund should never be exited. Funds should be reviewed.

But “my fund hasn't given good returns for two years” and “this investment is fundamentally no longer suitable for me” are two very different reasons for making a change.

## 4. Stop obsessing over the SIP date

Should the SIP happen on the 1st?

The 5th? The 15th? The 25th?

It is one of those questions investors love discussing.

Gajendra's response was straightforward. If you are investing over 10 or 20 years, you will accumulate so many purchase points that the particular date of the month is unlikely to make a meaningful difference.

In fact, SIP exists partly so that you **don't** have to keep timing the market. If your salary arrives on the first, investing soon after is perfectly reasonable.

There are bigger questions to solve , particularly whether you are saving enough in the first place.

## 5. Starting an SIP is only half the job. You also need an exit plan.

Most conversations about investing focus on how to start.

But suppose you have been investing for your child's education for 15 years and the money is required next year. What happens if equities fall 30% just before you need the corpus?

If a goal has a relatively fixed date and you are already close to the required amount, start moving some of the money towards lower-risk assets before the actual deadline.

You don't have to wait until the final month. Retirement is different.

Most retirees do not withdraw their entire retirement corpus on day one. They withdraw gradually while much of the money continues to remain invested. So the exit strategy should depend on the goal.

A daughter's wedding, college fees, buying a house and retirement don't necessarily require the same investment journey simply because they all involve money.

## 6. There is no permanently “best” fund

This brings us back to the most searched question in mutual funds.

**Which is the best mutual fund?** The problem is that the answer can only be known retrospectively.

A fund that topped the rankings over the last three years may not top them over the next ten.

Even the fund manager cannot know with certainty whether their fund will be number one a decade from now. Gajendra uses a nice cricket analogy. If someone gives you Sachin Tendulkar's bat, will you score a century?

Of course not, You would still need to become Sachin. Investing is similar.

The fund matters, but so do patience, discipline, behaviour, asset allocation and your ability to sit through uncomfortable periods. That is why perhaps the more useful question isn't:

**“Which is the best mutual fund?”** It is **“Am I becoming a better investor?”**

## 7. A good advisor is not just a fund selector

Another part of our conversation that stood out was Gajendra's view on financial advice. Investors often judge an advisor by asking: **Did he give me the highest-returning fund?**

But an investor's financial life is much wider than that. Should you take a larger home loan? How much should you save? Are you on track for retirement? Should you reduce equity before an important goal? Are you taking decisions because of fear? Are you reacting to every WhatsApp forward about markets?

Gajendra believes much of an advisor's value comes after the plan has already been created.

The job is often to make sure the investor doesn't abandon that plan whenever markets, headlines or emotions change. That is perhaps an underrated part of financial advice. Information is everywhere today. Sticking to sensible decisions for twenty years is still difficult.

## Maybe investing doesn't need to be so complicated

My biggest takeaway from this conversation wasn't a fund recommendation.

It was almost the opposite. We spend a lot of time trying to make investing clever.

Finding alpha. Timing markets. Identifying the next winning category. Comparing our returns with someone else's. But wealth creation can be surprisingly ordinary.

Save consistently. Invest enough. Increase the amount as your income rises. Give the money time. Review the portfolio against your goals. And don't make a new decision every time markets make you uncomfortable. Perhaps there really isn't a magical “best fund”. The harder task is becoming the kind of investor who can allow a good investment plan to work.
