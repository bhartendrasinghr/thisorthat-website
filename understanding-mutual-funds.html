Understanding your mutual funds.

Read any fund in three questions.

A fund name tells you almost nothing. A fund's box tells you nearly everything: what it is allowed to buy, what it is measured against, and how badly it has hurt people before. Everything on this page hangs off these three.

01

What box is it in?

The category sets hard rules the fund manager cannot break: how much equity, which sizes of company, how long the bonds can be. Rules, not promises.

02

What is it measured against?

Every scheme must declare a benchmark. Beating it is the whole job. If a fund brags about returns without naming its benchmark, that is the tell.

03

How hard can it fall?

The number that decides whether you stay invested long enough to get the returns. Growth is the reward. The fall is the price of entry.

What a benchmark actually is.

A benchmark is the scoreboard. It is a basket of the same kind of things the fund is allowed to buy, tracked without a manager. If the fund cannot beat that basket over a full cycle, you are paying a fee for the privilege of doing worse than a machine. SEBI makes every scheme declare one, and the fund cannot change it quietly.

The three letters that matter

TRI

Total Return Index. It counts the dividends the companies paid, reinvested, not just the share price. A price-only index looks lower than reality, which makes any fund measured against it look better than it is. Since 2018 funds must use TRI, which quietly ended a lot of flattering maths.

If you see a fund compared to a plain price index anywhere, that comparison is doing the fund a favour.

The numbers in index names

100, 150, 250, 500

Companies are ranked by market value twice a year. That ranking is the whole naming system:

  • 1 to 100 = large cap, so Nifty 100
  • 101 to 250 = mid cap, so Nifty Midcap 150, which is 150 companies
  • 251 onwards = small cap, so Nifty Smallcap 250
  • 1 to 500 = Nifty 500, most of the listed market

A ratio like 50:25:25 just means the index was re-weighted to hold that split of large, mid and small.

The code on every debt benchmark

A-I, A-II, A-III, B-II

Debt benchmarks carry a two part risk code. The letter is credit risk, meaning the chance a borrower does not pay you back. The roman numeral is interest rate risk, meaning how much the price moves when rates change. Read them together and you know what a debt fund can do to you before you read a single return.

Letter, credit risk
AHighest credit quality, the safest borrowers
BModerate credit risk, more yield, more chance of trouble
CRelatively high credit risk
Numeral, interest rate risk
ILowest, very short maturities
IIModerate
IIIHighest, long or unconstrained maturities
So A-I is the calmest corner of debt: safest borrowers, shortest paper. B-II, the credit risk benchmark, tells you plainly that you are taking on weaker borrowers to earn more. Neither is wrong. They are just different jobs.
The word in every debt category rule

Macaulay duration

The average wait, in years, to get your money back from a bond portfolio, counting every interest payment along the way. It is the closest thing to a speedometer for rate risk. As a rough rule of thumb, if rates rise by 1%, a portfolio with a duration of 5 loses roughly 5% of its value, and gains about that much if rates fall. That is why SEBI defines most debt categories by duration bands rather than by fund names: the band is the risk.

One naming trap

Hybrid index names are written debt first.

CRISIL Hybrid 85+15 Conservative is 85% debt and 15% equity, not the other way round. CRISIL Hybrid 35+65 Aggressive is 35% debt and 65% equity. The first number is the quiet half. Read it backwards and you will badly misjudge what you own.

The full map

Every way to own equity in India.

A mutual fund is one door into equity. There are of them, and they are not interchangeable. What separates them is not returns, it is what you actually end up holding, what it costs to get in, and how easily you can get out. Grouped by what lands in your name at the end.

Minimum ticket sizes are indicative and set by regulation or by the provider, and they change. Confirm the current figure before acting. This is a map of what exists, not a recommendation of any route.

Sold alongside, regulated apart

These are not mutual funds.

Three products get offered in the same conversation as mutual funds, often by the same person, and they are not mutual funds. Different rulebook, different minimum, different tax treatment. None of this makes them worse. It makes them a different decision, and the sales pitch rarely says so.

PMS

Min ₹50 L
Portfolio Management Services

You own the shares themselves, in your own demat account. No units, no NAV. A manager runs the account under your name.

  • Tax is yours, per trade. Every sale the manager makes is a taxable event for you. Inside a mutual fund it is not.
  • Fewer limits. Mutual funds face hard caps on how much can sit in one stock or sector. PMS has far more room, which is both the appeal and the risk.
  • Fees are higher, usually a fixed fee plus a share of the gains.
  • Comparing is harder. No single NAV, and returns are reported per SEBI and APMI rules rather than as one published series.

AIF

Min ₹1 Cr
Alternative Investment Fund

A pooled fund like a mutual fund, but for strategies mutual funds are not allowed to run, across three categories from venture and infrastructure through to long-short and derivative-led funds.

  • Money is usually locked for years, with no daily redemption.
  • Tax depends on the category, and it is not the simple equity treatment most people assume.
  • Meant for investors who can afford the ticket and the illiquidity, not as a core holding.

SIF

Min ₹10 L
Specialised Investment Fund

The newest of the three, designed to sit in the gap between a mutual fund and PMS: more strategy freedom than a mutual fund, a far smaller ticket than PMS, run by existing fund houses.

  • Still a pooled vehicle, so you hold units, not the shares themselves.
  • Wider strategies than the categories above, including long-short approaches.
  • Young. There is little track record to judge yet, so treat any pitch built on projections carefully.

Minimum ticket sizes are the regulatory floors as we understand them and do change, so confirm the current number and the product's own disclosure document before committing. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy any of them.

Volatility, and the number that actually matters.

The riskometer

Every scheme publishes a risk label each month, from low to very high. It is a useful first glance and a poor second one: it compresses a thirty year holding into one word, and most equity funds land on the same word. The labels on this page are the category norm, and your scheme publishes its own.

Worst 12 months

The one we lead with. Not because it is likely, but because it is survivable only if you have seen it in advance. Standard deviation describes a wobble. A drawdown describes the moment you open the app, see a number that hurts, and decide whether to sell. That decision, not the wobble, sets your actual return.

So, return chaser or drawdown survivor?

Here is our side of the fork: pick your category by the worst 12 months column, not the growth column. Growth is what the market gives everyone who stays. The drawdown decides whether you stay. If a 60% fall would make you sell, that category's long run 18% was never yours to begin with.

This is not a no, it is a not-like-this. If a box scares you, that is useful information, not a failing. Move down a band and stay invested, which beats picking the brave box and leaving at the bottom.
